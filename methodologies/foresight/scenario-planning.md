# Scenario Planning

> **Essence:** Build a small set of internally consistent, plausible and challenging stories about how the future environment could unfold, then use them to "wind-tunnel" decisions so strategy works across more than one future.

## Overview

Scenario planning is the flagship method of strategic foresight. It does not attempt to *predict* the future; instead it rejects single-point forecasting for environments dominated by uncertainty, and constructs several divergent but plausible futures (typically 2–4) that bound the space of what could happen. A decision, strategy, or policy is then tested against all of them: options that perform acceptably across every scenario are "robust"; options that win big in one future and fail in another are "bets" that need hedges or signposts.

The method's unit of work is the **scenario**: not a forecast, not a vision of a desired future, but an internally consistent narrative of how a set of driving forces could interact to produce a distinct future context. Scenarios are learning instruments — their purpose is to change the mental models of decision-makers (Pierre Wack called this "the gentle art of reperceiving"), not to be right.

Scenario planning is most powerful when the question is strategic, the time horizon is 5–30 years, the external environment dominates outcomes, and the organization can name at least two high-impact, high-uncertainty forces it cannot control.

## Origin & History

- **1950s–60s — military origins.** Herman Kahn at RAND and later the Hudson Institute used "future-now" thinking and scenario narratives to make nuclear strategy discussable ("thinking about the unthinkable", 1962). Kahn & Wiener's *The Year 2000* (1967) systematized scenario construction.
- **Late 1960s–70s — Shell.** Pierre Wack and Ted Newland built scenario planning into Royal Dutch/Shell's planning process. The 1971–72 scenario work anticipated the possibility of a producer-driven oil-price shock *before* the 1973 crisis, letting Shell react faster than competitors. Wack's two 1985 Harvard Business Review articles ("Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead", "Scenarios: Shooting the Rapids") are the classic accounts.
- **1980s — codification.** Arie de Geus (*The Living Company*, 1997) framed scenarios as organizational learning. The "intuitive logics" school (Ian Wilson; later Kees van der Heijden, *Scenarios: The Art of Strategic Conversation*, 1996) formalized the uncertainty-ranking method.
- **1987 — GBN.** Stewart Brand, Peter Schwartz, Napier Collyns, Jay Ogilvy and Lawrence Wilkinson founded Global Business Network. Schwartz's *The Art of the Long View* (1991) remains the standard practitioner text and source of the canonical 8-step process.
- **1990s–present — civic and policy use.** Mont Fleur scenarios for post-apartheid South Africa (Adam Kahane, 1991–92), Destino Colombia, Singapore's whole-of-government scenario programme (Centre for Strategic Futures), and widespread corporate and NGO adoption. Parallel school: Michel Godet's French *prospective* with structural analysis (see [cross-impact-analysis.md](cross-impact-analysis.md)).

## Core Concepts & Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Scenario | An internally consistent, plausible story about a future context in which decisions must be made — not a prediction. |
| Focal issue / decision question | The strategic question the exercise serves; everything orbits it. |
| Driving force | A force in the macro-environment (social, technological, economic, environmental, political) that shapes outcomes. |
| Predetermined element | A driving force whose outcome is essentially known (e.g., demographics already in the population). |
| Critical uncertainty | A driving force that is both high-impact and unpredictable; the raw material of scenario logic. |
| Scenario logic | The structural skeleton of a scenario set, often a 2×2 matrix from the two most critical uncertainties. |
| Wild card | A low-probability, high-impact event (pandemic, breakthrough, collapse) used to stress the set. |
| Signpost / indicator | An observable event that signals which scenario is beginning to unfold; monitored after the exercise. |
| Wind-tunnelling | Testing a strategy or option against every scenario for robustness. |
| Predetermined vs uncertainty map | Wack's discipline: separate what you can know from what you cannot, and plan around both. |

## When to Use / When Not to Use

**Use when:**
- The decision horizon is long (5–30 years) and the environment is genuinely uncertain.
- Leadership mental models are the bottleneck (groupthink, "official future" capture).
- You must choose investments/policies that cannot easily be reversed or hedged later.
- Multiple stakeholders need a shared language for disagreement about the future.

**Don't use when:**
- The problem is short-term, operational, or dominated by a few well-measured variables (use forecasting, trend analysis, or experiments).
- The organization wants a single answer — scenarios produce options and robustness judgments, not *the* plan.
- Leadership will not commit time to sense-making; scenarios without a decision process are just stories.
- You need probability estimates — classic scenario planning deliberately resists assigning odds (see [bayesian-evidence-updating.md](../scientific-methods/bayesian-evidence-updating.md) if probabilities are the goal).

## Process & Steps

The Schwartz/GBN 8-step process (workshop-based, typically 2–8 weeks elapsed):

1. **Identify the focal issue or decision.** One clear question from the decision-maker ("Should we enter market X by 2035?", "How must our university change by 2040?"). *Artifact: framing memo.*
2. **Identify key factors in the local environment.** What will success or failure of the decision hinge on that the organization can influence? *Artifact: key-factor list.*
3. **Identify and research driving forces in the macro-environment.** Scan widely (STEEP categories — see [steep-pestle-analysis.md](steep-pestle-analysis.md), [horizon-scanning.md](horizon-scanning.md)). *Artifact: driving-force inventory (typically 40–100 raw forces).*
4. **Rank driving forces by importance and uncertainty.** Two axes: impact on the focal question, and degree of unpredictability. Predetermined elements are separated out — they appear in *all* scenarios. *Artifact: impact/uncertainty map.*
5. **Select the scenario logics.** Choose the two (occasionally three) most critical uncertainties as axes of a 2×2 matrix; check the four quadrants produce meaningfully different, plausible worlds. *Artifact: scenario matrix with named quadrants.*
6. **Flesh out the scenarios.** For each quadrant, write a rich narrative (1–3 pages): how the world got there from today, what daily life and the industry look like, who wins/loses, early moments (newspaper-headline exercises). Give each scenario a memorable name. *Artifact: 3–4 scenario narratives + comparison table.*
7. **Draw implications.** Wind-tunnel current and candidate strategies against every scenario: robust moves, scenario-specific bets, options to keep open, hedges. *Artifact: implications matrix (option × scenario).*
8. **Select leading indicators and signposts.** Define observable signals per scenario and a monitoring rhythm so the organization knows which future is arriving. *Artifact: signpost dashboard (feeds back into [horizon-scanning.md](horizon-scanning.md)).*

**Workshop parameters:** 8–20 participants, diverse across functions/levels; 1–3 days of workshops plus pre-research; a neutral facilitator; no hierarchy inside the room.

## Techniques, Tools & Deliverables

- **2×2 matrix** (intuitive logics) — the default scenario-logic generator; guard against trivial axes ("optimistic/pessimistic" is a failure).
- **Scenario archetypes** — Dator's four generic futures (continued growth, collapse, disciplined society, transformation) as coverage check.
- **Headline exercise** — "Write the newspaper front page of this world in year T" to force concreteness.
- **Consistency checks** — cross-impact analysis ([cross-impact-analysis.md](cross-impact-analysis.md)) to test that a scenario's elements can co-occur.
- **Software** — any collaborative canvas (Miro/Mural) for workshops; matrix/impact tooling optional.
- **Deliverables:** scenario narratives, matrix poster, implications/options table, signpost list, monitoring plan.

## Strengths & Limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Handles deep uncertainty that forecasting cannot | Resource-heavy (weeks, senior people's time) |
| Changes mental models; legitimizes dissent | Quality depends entirely on framing and facilitation |
| Produces robust, hedged strategies | No probabilities — risk of "all futures are equal" |
| Reusable assets (signposts, options) across planning cycles | Stories can be memorable in the wrong way (one scenario becomes the de-facto forecast) |
| Works for nations, industries, NGOs, products | Hard to measure success; benefits are long-term and diffuse |

The best-documented misuse is the scenario that becomes a forecast: one of the four stories is more vivid, the room adopts it as "the future," and the hedging value the exercise was built for evaporates. The second is framing failure — axes chosen too close to today's debate produce four versions of the present. And the honest historical note: Shell's famous 1970s success is real but exceptional; many scenario programs produced beautiful booklets no decision ever used, because no one wired the signposts into a planning cycle. A scenario set without a monitoring ritual and a pre-agreed "if signpost X fires, we do Y" is literature, not planning.

## Worked Examples & Case Studies

- **Royal Dutch/Shell, 1971–73.** Wack's team presented scenarios in which OPEC producers gained leverage and prices could spike. When the 1973 crisis hit, Shell had already rehearsed the world and out-adapted rivals; scenario planning became institutionalized at Shell for decades.
- **Mont Fleur, South Africa, 1991–92.** A diverse group (politicians, business, unions, academics) built four scenarios for the transition ("Ostrich", "Lame Duck", "Icarus", "Flight of the Flamingos"). Widely credited with creating shared language across antagonists during the negotiated transition.
- **Singapore.** The Scenario Planning Office (now Centre for Strategic Futures) has run national scenarios since the 1990s, embedding futures thinking into civil-service planning and risk/horizon-scanning systems.

## Agent Adaptation

### Suitability for agent execution

**High** — with a human decision-maker in the loop. Research-heavy steps (driving-force gathering, clustering, narrative drafting, signpost design, wind-tunnelling) are exactly what agent pipelines do well. The irreducibly human parts are step 1 (choosing the focal decision — an act of accountability) and the final commitment to strategy. Beware: agents converge to plausible-sounding consensus; the Critic role is mandatory to keep scenarios *challenging*, not just coherent.

### Recommended multi-agent workflow

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Scout | Gather driving forces from web/sources across STEEP categories; deliver sourced force inventory. |
| Analyst | Deduplicate, cluster, and rate forces on impact × uncertainty; propose axis candidates. |
| Domain Expert (×2–4 personas) | Argue for different axis choices and quadrant interpretations from distinct disciplinary standpoints. |
| Critic / Red Team | Attack axis choices (trivial? correlated? normative?) and each narrative (inconsistencies, wishful thinking, hidden assumptions). |
| Synthesizer | Draft and refine the 3–4 scenario narratives, implications matrix, and signpost list. |
| Verifier | Check factual claims inside narratives (dates, statistics, causal claims) against sources. |
| Facilitator (human) | Set the focal question, approve axes, own the final strategy decision. |

### Agent pipeline

1. Frame (human) → focal question memo.
2. Gather (Scout, parallel per STEEP category) → `forces.json` [{force, category, description, source_urls}].
3. Analyze (Analyst) → cluster + score → `force_ratings.csv` (impact 1–5, uncertainty 1–5, rationale) + top-10 critical uncertainties + predetermined elements.
4. Debate (Domain Experts + Critic, 1–2 rounds) → 2–3 candidate axis pairs with arguments → human picks one.
5. Synthesize (Synthesizer, one narrative per quadrant, then Critic pass, then Verifier pass) → 4 narratives + consistency fixes.
6. Stress-test (Critic) → wild-card injection: does each scenario survive a shock? Patch or note fragility.
7. Report (Synthesizer) → implications matrix + signposts + monitoring plan → human strategy session.

### Prompt templates

```text
SYSTEM: You are the Scout agent in a scenario-planning exercise. Focal question: "{{focal_question}}".
Time horizon: {{horizon}} years. Scan authoritative, recent sources and identify driving forces in
the category {{steep_category}} that could materially change the context of this question.
Return JSON: [{"force": ..., "description": ..., "trend_or_uncertainty": "predetermined|uncertain",
"evidence": ..., "source_urls": [...]}]. At least 10 forces. Prefer primary and official sources.
Never invent statistics; if you cannot source a number, omit it.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are the Critic in a scenario-planning exercise. Here are the two proposed axes:
{{axis_1}} / {{axis_2}}. Attack them: Are they truly independent? Are they genuine uncertainties
or disguised preferences? Would the quadrants differ in ways that matter for "{{focal_question}}"?
Is one axis just "good vs bad"? Propose one alternative axis pair that would produce more
decision-relevant contrast, and defend it in 5 sentences.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are the Synthesizer. Write a scenario narrative for the quadrant "{{quadrant_name}}"
where {{axis_1_state}} and {{axis_2_state}}. World year: {{target_year}}. Constraints: all
predetermined elements hold ({{predetermined_list}}); the narrative must explain a causal path
from {{current_year}} to {{target_year}} in 3-4 phases; include one sector-specific consequence
for {{industry}} and one second-order surprise. 600-900 words. End with 5 observable signposts.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are the wind-tunnelling analyst. Strategy option: "{{option}}". Scenarios:
{{scenario_summaries}}. Score the option in each scenario (Fails / Survives / Thrives) with a
2-sentence justification each, then recommend: adopt / adapt / hedge / reject, and name the
earliest signpost that would tell us which scenario is unfolding.
```

### Tools & data requirements

Web search (Scout/Verifier), document retrieval for the organization's strategy papers, a shared structured store (JSON/CSV) for forces and ratings, optional code execution for consistency matrices.

### Quality checks, failure modes & mitigations

| Failure mode | Detection | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Axes collapse to good/bad or certain trends | Quadrants differ only in tone | Critic gate before narratives; require axes to be rated high-uncertainty in step 3 |
| Inter-agent groupthink (all scenarios converge) | Low pairwise divergence between narratives | Independent drafting per quadrant; Critic rewards challenge, penalizes echo |
| Hallucinated facts inside narratives | Verifier citation check fails | All empirical claims must carry sources; unverifiable claims rewritten or cut |
| The "official future" sneaks back | One scenario reads as baseline | Facilitator checks no scenario is labeled expected/probable |
| Beautiful stories, no decisions | Implications matrix empty | Hard gate: no sign-off until every option has a robust/hedge/bet verdict |

### Human-in-the-loop checkpoints

1. **Focal question** (start) — accountability for what the exercise serves.
2. **Axis selection** (step 5) — the creative act; humans choose, agents advise.
3. **Narrative approval** — leadership must recognize the worlds as plausible *and* uncomfortable.
4. **Strategy commitment** — robust/adapt/bet decisions are owned by decision-makers.

### Inputs & outputs (chaining contract)

**Inputs:** focal question + horizon (human); driving-force inventory (from [horizon-scanning.md](horizon-scanning.md), [steep-pestle-analysis.md](steep-pestle-analysis.md)); predetermined elements (from [trend-analysis.md](trend-analysis.md)).
**Outputs:** scenario narratives, implications/options matrix, signpost watchlist (feeds horizon scanning), identified robust actions (feeds [backcasting.md](backcasting.md) if a preferred scenario is chosen as vision).
