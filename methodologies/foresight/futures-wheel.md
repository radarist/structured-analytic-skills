# Futures Wheel

> **Essence:** A structured brainstorming technique that maps the direct (first-order), indirect (second-order), and higher-order consequences of a change — an event, trend, decision, or technology — as concentric rings around a central statement.

## Overview

The futures wheel is the foresight field's simplest tool for fighting first-order thinking. Place the change in the center of a page ("Widespread remote work", "EU bans new combustion cars in 2035", "We launch product X"). Ask: "If this happens, what happens **directly**?" — those answers form the inner ring (**first-order implications**). Then ask of *each* first-order implication: "and then what follows?" — producing the **second-order ring**; repeat for a **third ring**. The result is a radial mind-map of cascading consequences that routinely surprises its authors: second- and third-order effects (on real estate, family structures, supply chains, regulation, mental health) are where the real strategic content lives, and where unaided intuition stops.

The method's discipline is the chain rule: every outer-ring item must connect causally to a *specific* inner item ("because A, therefore possibly B"), not merely be "related to the topic". Wheels can be drawn for a single change or used to stress-test each scenario in a set ([scenario-planning.md](scenario-planning.md)), each option in a decision, or each signal in [horizon-scanning.md](horizon-scanning.md).

It is fast (20–60 minutes per wheel), works with any group, and produces a visual artifact that makes complexity discussable. Its weakness is the flip side of its openness: unconstrained wheels explode combinatorially, mix certainties with speculations, and stop wherever participants' imagination stops — hence the value of rating, and of agent-assisted breadth with human prun ing.

## Origin & History

- Invented by **Jerome C. Glenn** in 1971 as a futures-thinking teaching tool (Glenn later co-founded The Millennium Project). Glenn's own account: "The Futures Wheel" in *Futures Research Methodology* (Glenn & Gordon, eds., Millennium Project, multiple editions).
- Adopted widely in futures education, policy workshops, and strategic planning from the 1970s onward; often paired with trend analysis and scenario stress-testing.
- Digital variants (mind-mapping tools, collaborative canvases) and cross-impact-informed weighting have extended it; the method itself is unchanged because its strength is procedural simplicity.

## Core Concepts & Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Central statement | The change/event/decision being explored — stated as a definite occurrence ("X happens"), not a topic. |
| First-order implication | A direct, immediate consequence of the central statement. |
| Second-/third-order implication | A consequence of a consequence (of a consequence) — the rings where insight concentrates. |
| Branch | A chain of implications from center outward along one causal path. |
| Desirable / undesirable tagging | Marking implications by valence to surface policy needs (amplify/mitigate). |
| Wild-card ring | Optional outer ring for low-probability, high-impact branches. |
| Convergence point | Where multiple branches imply the same outcome — a robust consequence worth attention. |

## When to Use / When Not to Use

**Use when:**
- A specific change is on the table and you need its full consequence space, fast.
- Stress-testing scenarios, policies, product launches, or weak signals for side effects.
- Teaching second-order thinking to a team that keeps stopping at the obvious.
- Opening a workshop: wheels surface the group's tacit model of how the world works.

**Don't use when:**
- The question is about likelihood or timing (wheels say nothing about probability — pair with [delphi-method.md](delphi-method.md) or [bayesian-evidence-updating.md](../scientific-methods/bayesian-evidence-updating.md)).
- You need rigorous system dynamics (feedback loops, stocks/flows — use causal-loop/system-dynamics modeling; wheels are trees, not loop maps).
- The group will treat the map as analysis rather than as raw material for analysis.
- Very large changes with no focal statement ("the future of AI") — narrow the statement first.

## Process & Steps

1. **Write the central statement** as a definite event/decision/trend in a circle, center page. Precision matters: "30% of our workforce fully remote by 2028", not "remote work". *Artifact: focal statement.*
2. **Generate first-order implications.** "If this is true, what happens directly?" Aim for 5–10; each in its own bubble, ring 1. Push past the obvious (ask per domain: costs? behaviors? who loses? what's newly possible?). *Artifact: ring 1.*
3. **Generate second-order implications** for EACH first-order item ("and because of that, what?"). 2–5 per parent. *Artifact: ring 2.*
4. **Generate third-order implications** likewise. Stop at ring 3 for workshops (returns diminish and readability collapses). *Artifact: ring 3.*
5. **Review and prune.** Check every link: is the causal chain explicit and defensible? Delete non-sequiturs; mark contested links. *Artifact: cleaned wheel.*
6. **Assess.** Tag implications desirable/undesirable/neutral; mark high-impact items; circle convergence points (implications reached by multiple branches). *Artifact: assessed wheel.*
7. **Draw out actions.** For top items: what would we do to amplify, mitigate, monitor, or research further? Feed significant consequences into scenarios/strategy/risk processes. *Artifact: implication-action table.*

**Workshop parameters:** 3–12 people, 45–90 minutes; individuals brainstorm silently first, then aggregate — group wheels without silent pre-work anchor on the first ideas spoken.

## Techniques, Tools & Deliverables

- Paper/whiteboard or any mind-mapping/canvas tool (Miro, Mural, XMind).
- Silent-brainstorm-then-merge to fight anchoring.
- Color coding: valence (green/red), certainty (solid/dashed), or domain (STEEP letters on bubbles — see [steep-pestle-analysis.md](steep-pestle-analysis.md)).
- "So what?" passes: force every outer bubble through the causal-chain test aloud.
- **Deliverables:** the wheel graphic, assessed implication list, implication-action table.

## Strengths & Limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Fast, zero-training, works with any group | Combinatorial explosion; quality depends on facilitation/pruning |
| Reliably surfaces non-obvious consequences | No probabilities or timing — pure consequence topology |
| Visual artifact makes complexity discussable | Chains are plausible-sounding, not validated; can launder speculation |
| Excellent stress-test companion to scenarios | Groupthink risk if not run with silent generation first |
| Cheap to repeat per option/scenario | Depth and breadth limited by participants' imagination |

The method's quiet failure mode is laundering speculation: a chain of consequences written in a confident hand *reads* like analysis, but every arrow in the wheel is an unvalidated hypothesis. Two disciplines keep it honest — generate silently before sharing (groupthink otherwise collapses the wheel onto the first speaker's associations), and mark every branch as hypothesis-to-be-checked rather than finding. Also be candid about evidence: the futures wheel is widely taught and widely run, but published outcome evaluations of its predictive value are thin — its demonstrated value is in broadening the consequence search, not in getting the future right. Prune aggressively; a wheel with 60 unpruned branches impresses nobody who has to act on it.

## Worked Examples & Case Studies

- **Futures education:** the wheel is the standard introductory exercise in futures courses worldwide (University of Houston, Turku, Manoa programmes) — e.g., wheels on "a permanent settlement on Mars" or "universal basic income" used to teach higher-order thinking (documented in Glenn's *Futures Research Methodology* chapter).
- **Policy workshops:** used by government foresight units (e.g., in UK Foresight project workshops) to unpack implications of emerging technologies before scenario work.
- **Corporate stress-testing:** common facilitation tool for "what happens if we do X" sessions — pricing changes, platform shifts, AI adoption — though usually undocumented by name in public sources.

## Agent Adaptation

### Suitability for agent execution

**High — arguably the best ratio of agent-value to method-simplicity in foresight.** The wheel's bottleneck is human imagination breadth; a Scout/Analyst fleet can generate 10× the implications across more domains (STEEP coverage per ring) than a room of executives, and never tires at ring 3. The essential human steps are pruning, causal-chain judgment, and deciding which consequences matter. Risk to manage: agents produce fluent, plausible chains indiscriminately — enforce the chain rule and a Critic pass, or the wheel becomes plausible-sounding noise.

### Recommended multi-agent workflow

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Analyst (×N, one per STEEP domain or per first-order branch) | Generate ring-by-ring implications with explicit causal links. |
| Domain Expert (personas) | Add domain-depth branches (legal, technical, behavioral). |
| Critic / Red Team | Enforce the chain rule; delete non-sequiturs; mark speculation vs grounded inference; find missing negative consequences. |
| Synthesizer | Assemble the wheel structure, tag valence, find convergence points. |
| Verifier | Where implications cite empirical facts (elasticities, precedents), check them. |
| Facilitator (human) | Owns the central statement, pruning, and the "what matters" assessment. |

### Agent pipeline

1. Frame (human) → precise central statement + ring budget (usually 3).
2. Generate (Analyst fleet, parallel per domain) → `ring1.json` then per-item `ring2.json`, `ring3.json` [id, parent_id, implication, chain_rationale, domain].
3. Stress-test (Critic) → chain-rule audit; speculation tagging; missing-consequence sweep.
4. Synthesize (Synthesizer) → wheel artifact (mermaid/mind-map), valence tags, convergence points.
5. Human session → prune, assess, draw actions.

### Prompt templates

```text
SYSTEM: You are an implication Analyst in a futures-wheel exercise. Central statement:
"{{central_statement}}". Your domain: {{steep_domain}}. Generate the FIRST-ORDER implications in
your domain: direct, immediate consequences. For each: the implication (one sentence), and the
causal chain in the form "because <central statement>, therefore possibly <implication>, since
<mechanism>". 4-8 implications. JSON: [{"implication": ..., "chain": ..., "mechanism": ...}].
No topic-adjacent ideas — only items that pass the chain test.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are an implication Analyst. Parent implication: "{{parent}}" (ring {{n}} of a futures
wheel on "{{central_statement}}"). Generate 2-5 NEXT-RING implications, each passing the test:
"because <parent>, therefore possibly <implication>, since <mechanism>". Include at least one
unintended or perverse consequence. JSON with parent_id references.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are the Critic auditing a futures wheel: {{wheel_json}}. For EVERY link, apply the
chain rule: is the parent-child connection causal and explicit, or merely thematic? Delete or
flag links that fail. Then run two sweeps: (1) missing negative consequences (the wheel is too
optimistic if >70% of items are desirable); (2) missing rings — which high-impact first-order
item was left unexplored? Output: flagged_links[], deletions[], missing_branches[].
```

### Tools & data requirements

No special tools needed for generation (pure reasoning); web search for Verifier when implications embed empirical claims; mind-map/mermaid rendering for the artifact.

### Quality checks, failure modes & mitigations

| Failure mode | Detection | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Thematic drift (children not caused by parents) | Critic chain audit | Chain-rule phrasing enforced in generation prompts |
| Explosion (200+ bubbles nobody reads) | Node count per ring | Ring budgets; human prunes with "top 3 per branch" rule |
| Optimism/pessimism skew | Valence distribution | Critic sweep mandates counter-valence items |
| Fluent speculation reads as fact | Verifier check on empirical claims | Tag grounded vs speculative explicitly on the wheel |
| Anchoring on first generation round | Similar trees across runs | Parallel independent generation, then merge |

### Human-in-the-loop checkpoints

1. Central statement wording (determines everything).
2. Pruning and assessment (what matters, what's actionable).
3. Actions and handoffs into strategy/scenario processes.

### Inputs & outputs (chaining contract)

**Inputs:** a precise central statement — a decision option, a scenario, a trend (from [trend-analysis.md](trend-analysis.md)), or an emerging issue (from [horizon-scanning.md](horizon-scanning.md)).
**Outputs:** assessed wheel, convergence points, implication-action table — feeding [scenario-planning.md](scenario-planning.md) (narrative texture, wild cards), risk registers, policy design.
