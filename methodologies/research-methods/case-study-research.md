# Case Study Research

> **Essence:** An in-depth empirical inquiry into a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context, using multiple sources of evidence — where the boundary between phenomenon and context is not clear — to explain, explore, or build theory.

## Overview

The case study is the method of choice when the question is *how* or *why* something happens in its real context and the researcher cannot control events (distinguishing it from experiments). "Case" can be an organization, a project, a decision, a community, a policy, an event — anything bounded. The method's credibility machinery (Yin) has four pillars: a **case study protocol** (pre-specified procedures, questions, and data plan — the project's operating manual); **multiple sources of evidence** (documents, archival records, interviews, direct observation, participant observation, artifacts) with **triangulation** across them; a **chain of evidence** (explicit links from conclusions back through analysis to raw data, so a reader can retrace any claim); and a **case study database** (the organized evidence store, separate from the report).

Design choices follow Yin: **single vs multiple case** (single for critical/unusual/revelatory cases; multiple for replication logic — each case is an *experiment-like* test of the emerging finding, with **literal replication** (similar results predicted) and **theoretical replication** (contrasting results predicted for theory-specified reasons)), and **holistic vs embedded** (one unit of analysis vs sub-units within the case). Analysis matches explanation-building, pattern-matching (compare empirically observed patterns to predicted/rival patterns), and cross-case synthesis.

Two complementary traditions matter in practice. **Eisenhardt** (management/IS): *building theory from cases* — iterative cycling between data and emerging constructs, cross-case comparison tables, and sharp construct definition; her 1989 ASQ paper is the standard playbook for inductive theory papers. **Stake**: *intrinsic* cases (studied for their own sake), *instrumental* cases (to understand something else), *collective* cases; emphasis on naturalistic generalization and the reader's experience.

## Origin & History

- Early 20th century: Chicago school sociology (urban ethnographies), clinical case tradition in medicine/psychology (Freud; later Bromley).
- **1984:** Robert K. Yin, *Case Study Research: Design and Methods* (Sage; many editions since) — the codification that made case study a rigorous, teachable method with explicit quality tests.
- **1989:** Kathleen M. Eisenhardt, "Building Theories from Case Study Research" (*Academy of Management Review*, 14(4)) — theory-building canon in management/IS.
- **1995:** Robert E. Stake, *The Art of Case Study Research* — the interpretive/naturalistic tradition.
- Standard critiques to know: Flyvbjerg's "Five Misunderstandings About Case-Study Research" (2006) defends the method against the "can't generalize" charge (analytic, not statistical, generalization).

## Core Concepts & Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Case | The bounded unit studied (organization, project, event, community...); bounding is a design act. |
| Unit of analysis | What the case is *of* (and embedded sub-units, if any). |
| Protocol | Pre-specified procedures, field questions, data plan — the reliability instrument. |
| Triangulation | Converging evidence from multiple sources/methods on the same finding. |
| Chain of evidence | Traceable path: conclusion → analysis → citations/notes → raw source. |
| Case study database | Organized evidence store (notes, documents, tables) separate from the narrative. |
| Literal / theoretical replication | Multiple-case logic: cases chosen to predict similar / contrasting results. |
| Pattern matching | Comparing observed patterns to predicted or rival patterns. |
| Analytic generalization | Generalizing to theory, not to populations. |
| Intrinsic / instrumental / collective case | Stake's purpose typology. |

## When to Use / When Not to Use

**Use when:**
- Questions are *how/why* about contemporary events you cannot control.
- Context is part of the phenomenon (implementation, adoption, failure, turnaround).
- You need theory from rich reality (Eisenhardt) or deep understanding of one bounded thing (Stake).
- Triangulating documents + interviews + observation is feasible.

**Don't use when:**
- Questions are *how many / how much* (surveys, archival statistics, experiments).
- You need statistical generalization to a population.
- Access is weak — case studies die without depth of data.
- "Case study" is being used as a license for an anecdote (the protocol/database/chain-of-evidence machinery is what separates research from storytelling).

## Process & Steps

Yin's design, with Eisenhardt's theory-building cycle noted:

1. **Define and design**: research questions (how/why), propositions (if any), unit(s) of analysis, case-selection logic (single critical/revelatory vs multiple replication), protocol. *Artifact: protocol.*
2. **(Eisenhardt) Enter with a defined focus but planned flexibility**; select cases by theoretical sampling, not convenience.
3. **Prepare and collect**: per case — documents, archival data, interviews (semi-structured), observation, artifacts; maintain the case database and field notes. *Artifact: case database per case.*
4. **Within-case analysis**: case narrative, coding (often GT-flavored — see [grounded-theory.md](grounded-theory.md)), explanation-building/pattern-matching against propositions and rivals. *Artifact: within-case report.*
5. **Cross-case analysis (multiple cases)**: replication logic — compare patterns across literal/theoretical replications; Eisenhardt's tactic: pick pairs/dimensions and tabulate similarities/differences to sharpen constructs. *Artifact: cross-case tables.*
6. **Shape theory (if building)**: iterate data ↔ constructs ↔ literature; compare with conflicting *and* similar literature (raises theoretical level and confidence). *Artifact: constructs, propositions, mid-range theory.*
7. **Validate**: quality tests — construct validity (multiple sources + chain of evidence + member review), internal validity (pattern matching, rival explanations), external validity (replication logic in design), reliability (protocol + database). *Artifact: validation notes.*
8. **Report**: case narratives + analysis + theory; keep the audit trail available.

## Techniques, Tools & Deliverables

- Protocol templates (Yin's structure: overview, field procedures, case questions, report guide).
- Evidence-source matrices (source × topic coverage).
- Cross-case comparison tables (Eisenhardt's signature tool).
- Explanation-building timelines/process maps for how/why questions.
- CAQDAS for coding; the case database as the audit spine.
- **Deliverables:** protocol, case database(s), within- and cross-case analyses, validated constructs/propositions or explanatory account, the report with chain of evidence.

## Strengths & Limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Depth: causal texture that surveys/experiments can't reach | Time-hungry; access-dependent |
| Triangulation + chain of evidence = auditable rigor | Generalization is analytic (to theory), not statistical — often misunderstood |
| Handles contemporary, uncontrollable phenomena | Researcher bias risk without protocol discipline |
| Builds *and* tests theory (pattern matching vs rivals) | Multiple-case designs multiply cost; single-case designs invite the "so what" critique |
| Flexible across epistemologies (Yin/Stake/Eisenhardt) | Label abuse: "case study" slapped on anecdotes |

The chronic failure is the label: a writeup of one project becomes "a case study" with none of the discipline — no case definition, no chain of evidence, no rival explanations considered. Yin's machinery exists precisely to force those three things, and skipping them produces anecdotes with citation counts. The "so what" critique of single cases is also fair and worth planning for: a single case earns its generalization through theory (analytic, not statistical), so the propositions must be stated before data collection or the case proves nothing beyond itself. Multiple-case designs multiply both cost and inferential power — choose on that tradeoff explicitly, not by budget accident.

## Worked Examples & Case Studies

- **Yin's exemplars:** the method text uses cases like community/organizational studies to demonstrate single-embedded and multiple-case designs (the textbook canon).
- **Eisenhardt & Bourgeois (1988), "Politics of Strategic Decision Making in High-Velocity Environments"** (*ASQ*): multiple-case study of microcomputer firms — the exemplar of theory-building from cases; outcome: a durable mid-range theory (politics managed through "consensus with qualification") that single-case work could not have supported.
- **Pettigrew's longitudinal case work** on organizational change (contextualist case research).
- **Flyvbjerg (2006)** — not a case, but the must-read defense of case methodology against the generalization critique.

## Variants & Related Methodologies

- **Single holistic / single embedded / multiple holistic / multiple embedded** (Yin's 2×2).
- **Theory-building from cases** (Eisenhardt); **extended case method** (Burawoy — theory reconstruction via cases).
- **Process tracing** (within-case causal-mechanism testing — political science's rigorous sibling).
- **Comparative case analysis / QCA** (Ragin — set-theoretic cross-case).

## Agent Adaptation

### Suitability for agent execution

**Medium.** Agents excel at the infrastructure of rigor: building and querying the case database, document analysis at scale, drafting within-case narratives from coded evidence, generating cross-case comparison tables, and enforcing the chain of evidence (every claim linked to sources). Agents cannot conduct the interviews or site observations — human fieldwork remains the data engine — and case selection + rival-explanation judgment need human ownership. Pattern: humans gather and adjudicate; agents organize, compare, draft, and audit.

### Recommended multi-agent workflow

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Human researcher | Fieldwork (interviews, observation), case selection, rival-explanation adjudication, theory decisions. |
| Scout | Collect public documents/archival material per case (with provenance). |
| Analyst | Code evidence, build source×topic matrices, draft within-case narratives, assemble cross-case tables. |
| Domain Expert (persona) | Propose rival explanations per case; contextual plausibility checks. |
| Critic / Red Team | Audit chain of evidence (claim→source traceability), triangulation coverage, pattern-matching vs rivals. |
| Synthesizer | Maintain the case database, comparison tables, and report skeleton. |
| Verifier | Confirm quotes/document claims against sources. |

### Agent pipeline

1. Design (human) → protocol (questions, case logic, data plan).
2. Collect (human fieldwork + Scout documents) → `case_db/<case>/` [source, type, date, provenance].
3. Analyze (Analyst) → coded evidence + within-case narrative drafts → human revision.
4. Compare (Analyst) → cross-case tables; Domain Expert + Critic → rival-pattern testing.
5. Theorize (human, with Synthesizer's construct tables) → propositions/explanations.
6. Audit (Critic) → chain-of-evidence spot checks; triangulation coverage report.
7. Report (Synthesizer drafts, human writes).

### Prompt templates

```text
SYSTEM: You are the case-database analyst. Case: {{case_description}}. Evidence corpus:
{{documents_and_transcripts}}. Build: (1) a source-by-topic matrix showing which evidence covers
which protocol question (and exposing UNCOVERED questions); (2) for each key event/claim, the
triangulation status: confirmed by >=2 independent sources / single-source / contradicted (list
the contradiction); (3) a timeline of events with source references. Output structured tables.
Every cell must cite source IDs.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are the rival-explanation engine. Within-case analysis: {{case_analysis}}. The
emerging explanation claims: {{claims}}. Generate the 3 strongest RIVAL explanations for the
case's outcome (different mechanism, same evidence). For each rival: what it predicts that the
current explanation doesn't (and vice versa), and what evidence in the case database discriminates
between them. Rank rivals by survivability against the existing evidence.
```

```text
SYSTEM: You are the cross-case analyst. Within-case summaries: {{within_case_summaries}}.
Emerging constructs: {{constructs}}. Build Eisenhardt-style comparison tables: (1) case x
construct evidence table (cell = evidence summary + source ref); (2) pair-wise similarity/
difference analysis on dimensions where cases were chosen for literal vs theoretical replication;
(3) sharpened construct definitions with measurement-relevant properties. Highlight cells where
evidence is thin or missing.
```

### Tools & data requirements

Document store with provenance metadata, CAQDAS or structured coding store, spreadsheet tables for matrices; no heavy computation; retrieval over the case database is the key capability.

### Quality checks, failure modes & mitigations

| Failure mode | Detection | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Claims without sources | Critic chain-of-evidence audit | Every analytical claim carries source IDs |
| Single-source "triangulation" | Triangulation status report | Findings tagged by support level; single-source claims flagged |
| Cherry-picked quotes | Verifier quote-in-context check | Quote + surrounding context audited on sample |
| Case selection convenience bias | Protocol vs actual selection diff | Selection rationale documented and audited |
| Agent narrative over-smooths contradictions | Critic contradiction report | Contradictions surfaced as findings, never resolved silently |
| Cross-case tables flatten cases | Human case-owner review | Within-case reports precede cross-case work |

### Human-in-the-loop checkpoints

1. Protocol and case selection.
2. Interview/observation conduct and interpretation.
3. Rival-explanation adjudication and theory decisions.
4. Final narrative voice (the report is an argument, owned by the researcher).
