# Phase indicators — checklists for `apply-hype-cycle`

Observable indicators for each of the five Hype Cycle phases. The phase names and the curve come from
Fenn & Raskino, *Mastering the Hype Cycle* (Harvard Business Press, 2008); the 26 indicators below, the
per-phase counts and the counting rule are this skill's own construction, written to make the placement
observable and re-derivable. Confirm an indicator only with a citation — a dated article,
a filing, a funding database entry, a published architecture, a named deployment. Count confirmed
indicators per phase; the phase with the most confirmations is the placement (step 3 of the skill).

An indicator that cannot be sourced is *not* a match. Absence of evidence is recorded as "not
confirmed", never as a negative match for another phase.

## 1 — Innovation Trigger

- A seminal paper, patent or public demonstration has appeared in roughly the last 6–24 months (a recency window for the indicator, not a dwell time).
- A handful of research groups or startups exist; no large-enterprise production deployments.
- Mainstream press coverage is thin; specialist press carries early explanatory pieces.
- Venture investment is nascent — a small number of funded companies, seed and Series A stages.
- Use cases are speculative: roadmaps and slideware rather than live customers.

## 2 — Peak of Inflated Expectations

- Repeated front-page coverage in the general business press.
- Vendor language turns absolute: "transformational", "revolutionary", "changes everything".
- Investment surges — a crowded field of funded companies and unusually large round sizes.
- Consulting and analyst houses publish very large market forecasts for a still-unproven category.
- Incumbents add the technology to roadmaps and rebrand existing products around it.
- Reference customers are mostly pilots or internal projects; few named commercial deployments.

## 3 — Trough of Disillusionment

- A high-profile deployment failure, cancellation or missed launch date is publicly reported.
- Consolidation: pivots, down-round acquisitions or shutdowns among the earlier entrants.
- Press turns sceptical — retrospectives asking what happened to the promised transformation.
- Magic-bullet claims are retracted, narrowed or quietly dropped from vendor materials.
- Second-generation products appear that deliberately *narrow* scope to a defensible use case.

## 4 — Slope of Enlightenment

- Reference architectures and implementation patterns are published and cited by third parties.
- Named customer case studies report measured outcomes, not just enthusiasm.
- Open-source or commercial implementations reach production quality; tooling matures.
- Second- and third-wave vendors ship specialised variants for particular verticals.
- Integration standards, benchmarks or certification schemes emerge; the discourse turns specific.

## 5 — Plateau of Productivity

- The technology is discussed as infrastructure rather than as news.
- A recognised job title or skill exists for working with it.
- Buying decisions turn on price, support and reliability rather than on capability claims.
- Analyst coverage shifts from "is this real?" to "which implementation and how".
- Adjacent emerging technologies cite it as an assumed building block.

## Stall and dropout

The published model does not guarantee that a technology traverses the curve. Dedehayir & Steinert
(2016) note that the shape is a stylised composite rather than an empirically validated trajectory,
and Gartner's own retrospectives show technologies that are dropped from the curve, obsoleted before
the plateau, or that sit in the Trough indefinitely. Record a stall risk whenever a technology has
been in the Trough for five or more years with no Slope indicators confirmed — a threshold set by this
skill, not a published Gartner figure.
